How to keep the phone ringing when money is tight

By Andrew TesauroCo-Founder & CEONovember 18, 2026

The short version: if things slow down, don't cut the things that make the phone ring. Cut the spend nobody can connect to a call. Most of what keeps a local business visible on Google is free, so start there. Full disclosure: I co-founded Bright Automations, and the last section is about where we fit.

A lot of economists are worried about 2027. Nobody knows for sure what happens next, but the owners we talk to are already asking the same thing: if money gets tight, what do I cut? This is the plan I'd give a friend who runs a one or two truck business. The free steps come first, in the order I'd do them.

Why cutting marketing first is usually the wrong call

When owners expect a downturn, marketing is the first thing most of them want to cut. In a Clarify Capital survey of 1,000 small business owners, 28% said marketing would be the first cost to go, more than any other expense, and only 10% called it off limits. The same survey found 46% planned to increase marketing or sales and 43% planned to build websites or online sales channels (MediaPost, eMarketer). Owners are torn, and that makes sense: you need customers, and you can't waste a dollar.

History leans one way. Harvard Business Review studied 4,700 public companies across three past recessions. The group most likely to come out ahead cut costs through efficiency while still spending more than their rivals on marketing (Gulati, Nohria and Wohlgezogen, HBR 2010). For a local business, I read that simply: cut waste, not customers.

Step 1: find the spend you can't connect to a call

Write down everything you pay for marketing each month: the agency, the directory listings, the ads, the software. Next to each one, write what it brought in last month. Calls, booked jobs, anything you can point to. Then ask every new caller one question: "How did you find us?" It costs nothing and it tells you more than most reports.

Anything you can't connect to a customer is the first line to cut. Anything that clearly pays for itself stays, even when money is tight. If you pay an agency, ask them the same question. If nobody can tell you what last month's spend produced, that's your answer. Published local SEO retainers run $500 to $3,000 a month, so it's worth asking (WebFX). I put agency prices side by side with ours in Bright Automations vs a typical marketing agency.

Step 2: fix your Google Business Profile (free)

For most local service businesses, the Google profile is where the calls start. It's free, and most owners set it up once and never touch it again. Spend an hour on it:

  1. Pick the primary category that matches the work you most want, not a broad one.
  2. List every service you actually do, in the words customers use.
  3. Set a service area that matches the towns you really work in.
  4. Add real photos of your jobs, your truck and your crew.
  5. Keep your hours current, including holidays.

Jared wrote a longer checklist for this in Your Google profile isn't getting calls? Check these 6 things first.

Step 3: ask every customer for a review, and answer every one (free)

Reviews are the cheapest marketing a local business has. Ask every happy customer, every time, not just the ones who gush. Text them the link right after the job while it's fresh. Then reply to every review, good or bad, in a calm, real voice. A thoughtful reply to a bad review often does more for the next customer than another five-star review does.

Step 4: make your website work on a phone (mostly free)

Most of your customers will find you on a phone, so check your own site on one. Can someone tap your number and call in one move? Is there a short contact form that works? Does the page say what you do and which towns you serve, right at the top? Does it load quickly? You can check all of that in five minutes.

Then look at how many pages you have. When we crawled 175 of our client sites, the sites with 10 or more pages got about twice the Google visitors. A page for each service and each town you work in gives Google more reasons to show you. Jared explains why in What 175 client sites taught us about getting found on Google.

Step 5: make your name, phone and website match everywhere (free)

Search your business name and check every listing that comes up: Google, Facebook, Yelp, Bing, any directory you've ever signed up for. The business name, phone number and website should match exactly. Old numbers and old addresses confuse customers and Google alike. Fixing them costs an afternoon.

Step 6: answer fast

The cheapest new customer is the one who already called you. When money is tight, a missed call is money left on the table. Call back the same day, reply to every text and form, and if you can't pick up on a job, have a plan for who follows up. None of this costs a dime, and it's often the biggest win on this list.

How can a small business market itself in a recession?

Start with what's free: a complete Google Business Profile, a review from every happy customer, a website that works on a phone, matching listings everywhere and fast replies to every call and text. Then cut any paid spend you can't connect to a customer, and keep what clearly pays for itself.

Should I cut marketing if money gets tight?

Cut waste, not customers. In Harvard Business Review's study of 4,700 companies across three past recessions, the group most likely to come out ahead cut costs through efficiency while still investing in marketing. Drop the spend nobody can tie to a call, and keep the things that make the phone ring.
